Find the Peak, Find the Formula

Job’s method of continuous variation

How many ligands does one metal ion hold? How many cyclodextrin rings wrap one drug molecule?

Oftentimes you cannot pull the complex out of solution to weigh it. Instead, mix equal-concentration solutions of the metal ion and ligand (or the cation and anion) in every proportion, keeping the total volume, and so the total concentration, the same in each mixture. Measure one property of each mixture and find the one that makes the most product. The mole ratio at the point of maximum product is the formula of the complex.1,5

\(x_{\text{L,max}} = \dfrac{n}{m+n}\)
\(x_{\text{L}}\)
mole fraction of ligand in the mixture, \(n_{\text{L}}/(n_{\text{M}}+n_{\text{L}})\)
\(x_{\text{L,max}}\)
mole fraction at which the signal is largest
\(m\)
number of metal (M) units in the complex \(\mathrm{M}_m\mathrm{L}_n\)
\(n\)
number of ligand (L) units in the complex
Read the peak position, invert the equation, and you have the combining ratio \(n/m\). A peak at 0.50 means M : L = 1:1, at 0.67 means 1:2, and at 0.75 means 1:3.

Section 1

Introduction to the method of continuous variation

The method of continuous variation, also called Job’s method or the Job plot, finds the combining ratio of two species that react or bind in solution. It is named after the French chemist Paul Job, who set it out in 1928 in a long study of how inorganic complexes form in solution.1 Similar ideas had appeared earlier in studies of liquid mixtures by Ostromisslensky (1911) and Denison (1912).2,3 In 1941 Vosburgh and Cooper showed how to use it when more than one complex can form,4 and it is still used across inorganic, organic, organometallic, supramolecular and biological chemistry.5,6

The idea in brief

Keep the total number of moles of the two partners constant, vary the proportion continuously from all M to all L, and plot a property that depends on how much product formed. The product is greatest where the reactants are mixed in exactly the ratio in which they combine.

An everyday analogy

You have 12 slices in total, some bread and some cheese, and each sandwich needs 2 bread + 1 cheese. With 6 bread + 6 cheese you make 3 sandwiches; with 10 bread + 2 cheese you make 2. The most sandwiches (4) come from 8 bread + 4 cheese, a cheese fraction of exactly 1/3. The “peak” gives the recipe. Try it in the drawing below.

Why it works

Away from the ideal ratio, one reactant runs out first and the other is left over, wasted. Only at the stoichiometric ratio are both used up together, so the amount of product, and any signal proportional to it, reaches its maximum.6,7

The experiment, step by step

  1. Make equimolar stock solutions of M and L (same concentration \(c_0\)), in the same buffer, solvent and ionic strength.
  2. Mix them in a series of proportions, for example \(V_{\text{M}} = 10, 9, 8, \dots, 0\) mL with \(V_{\text{L}} = 0, 1, 2, \dots, 10\) mL, so every mixture has the same total volume (10.00 mL).
  3. Let each mixture reach equilibrium at constant temperature.
  4. Measure a property that changes in proportion to the amount of complex: absorbance, fluorescence, an NMR shift, heat released, mass of precipitate, and so on (Section 3).
  5. Subtract what the unreacted M and L would contribute on their own.
  6. Plot the corrected signal against the mole fraction \(x_{\text{L}}\). Draw straight lines through the points near each end; where they meet (or where the curve peaks) gives the ratio.4,7

Mole fraction from volumes

With equimolar stocks and a fixed total volume, mole fraction is just volume fraction:7

\[ x_{\text{L}} = \frac{n_{\text{L}}}{n_{\text{M}} + n_{\text{L}}} = \frac{c_0 V_{\text{L}}}{c_0 V_{\text{M}} + c_0 V_{\text{L}}} = \frac{V_{\text{L}}}{V_{\text{T}}} \]
\[ x_{\text{M}} = 1 - x_{\text{L}} \]

For a 10.00 mL series, a mixture of 3.00 mL M + 7.00 mL L has \(x_{\text{L}} = 0.700\).

Section 2

How is the formula of a compound determined, and why is the method useful?

A Job plot gives the combining ratio of the two partners, \(n/m\). Turning that ratio into a formula takes one short derivation, a correction for anything that is not the complex, and, when it matters, a second measurement that tells you the size of the molecule.

Where the peak must be: the derivation

Let one complex form, with an overall formation constant \(\beta\):

\[ m\,\mathrm{M} + n\,\mathrm{L} \rightleftharpoons \mathrm{M}_m\mathrm{L}_n \qquad \beta = \frac{[\mathrm{M}_m\mathrm{L}_n]}{[\mathrm{M}]^{m}\,[\mathrm{L}]^{n}} \]

Write \(C = [\mathrm{M}_m\mathrm{L}_n]\) and \(x = x_{\text{M}}\). Mass balance at constant total concentration \(c_{\text{T}}\):

\[ [\mathrm{M}] = x c_{\text{T}} - mC \qquad [\mathrm{L}] = (1-x)c_{\text{T}} - nC \]

Take logarithms and differentiate with respect to \(x\):

\[ 0 = \frac{1}{C}\frac{dC}{dx} - \frac{m}{[\mathrm{M}]}\left(c_{\text{T}} - m\frac{dC}{dx}\right) - \frac{n}{[\mathrm{L}]}\left(-c_{\text{T}} - n\frac{dC}{dx}\right) \]

At the maximum \(dC/dx = 0\), so \(m/[\mathrm{M}] = n/[\mathrm{L}]\). Substituting the mass balances, the \(C\) terms cancel:6–8

\[ m(1-x_{\max}) = n\,x_{\max} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_{\text{M,max}} = \frac{m}{m+n}, \qquad x_{\text{L,max}} = \frac{n}{m+n} \]

\(\beta\) and \(c_{\text{T}}\) have dropped out. For a single complex, the peak position depends only on the formula, never on how strong the binding is or what total concentration you chose.8

\[ \frac{n}{m} = \frac{x_{\text{L,max}}}{1 - x_{\text{L,max}}} \]

Peaks for high ratios crowd together near \(x_{\text{L}} = 1\) (0.80, 0.83, 0.857), so distinguishing ML4 from ML5 or ML6 needs closely spaced points and a precise signal.5

Step 1 · Correct the signal

If M or L also contributes (for example, both absorb a little at the chosen wavelength), subtract what they would give with no reaction. For absorbance with path length \(b\):

\[ Y = A_{\text{obs}} - b\left(\varepsilon_{\text{M}} c_{\text{M}} + \varepsilon_{\text{L}} c_{\text{L}}\right) = b\left(\varepsilon_{\text{C}} - m\varepsilon_{\text{M}} - n\varepsilon_{\text{L}}\right)[\mathrm{M}_m\mathrm{L}_n] \]

The corrected signal \(Y\) is directly proportional to the amount of complex, which is what the derivation needs. The same logic works for any additive property (Section 3).4,6

Step 2 · Extract a formation constant (bonus)

The straight end lines show what complete reaction would give. They meet at \(A_{\text{ext}}\); the measured peak \(A_{\max}\) falls short because some complex dissociates. The fraction dissociated is

\[ \alpha = \frac{A_{\text{ext}} - A_{\max}}{A_{\text{ext}}} \]

For \(\mathrm{ML}_n\), with \(c = c_{\text{T}}/(1+n)\) the concentration of complex if formation were complete:7,8

\[ \beta_n = \frac{1-\alpha}{n^{n}\,\alpha^{\,n+1}\,c^{\,n}} \]

Worked example

A metal M and ligand L are mixed in a 10.00 mL continuous-variation series with \(c_{\text{T}} = 1.00\) mM and a 1.00 cm cell. After correction, the end lines meet at \(x_{\text{L}} = 0.667\) with \(A_{\text{ext}} = 0.600\); the measured absorbance there is \(A_{\max} = 0.540\).

\[ \frac{n}{m} = \frac{0.667}{1-0.667} = 2.00 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \mathrm{ML_2} \]
\[ c = \frac{c_{\text{T}}}{1+n} = \frac{1.00 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{M}}{3} = 3.33 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M} \qquad \varepsilon_{\text{C}} = \frac{A_{\text{ext}}}{bc} = \frac{0.600}{(1.00\ \text{cm})(3.33 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{M})} = 1.80 \times 10^{3}\ \text{M}^{-1}\,\text{cm}^{-1} \]
\[ \alpha = \frac{0.600 - 0.540}{0.600} = 0.100 \qquad \beta_2 = \frac{1 - 0.100}{2^{2}\,(0.100)^{3}\,(3.33 \times 10^{-4})^{2}} = 2.0 \times 10^{9}\ \text{M}^{-2} \]

One series of mixtures gives the formula, the molar absorptivity of a complex you never isolated, and an estimate of its stability.8

From ratio to molecular formula

The Job plot fixes the ratio \(n/m\). It cannot tell ML from M2L2, because both peak at 0.50. To write a molecular formula:

  1. Ratio from the peak (Job plot).
  2. Charge from the charges of M and L, so the complex balances: Fe2+ + 3 phen gives [Fe(phen)3]2+.
  3. Size (degree of aggregation) from a second method when it is in doubt: mass spectrometry, a colligative property, diffusion NMR, or an “ensemble” Job plot that counts subunits (Application 3).5,12,17
  4. Chemical sense: coordination number, denticity and geometry should be consistent with the formula.

Why the method is so useful

  • No isolation needed. It works on species that exist only in solution and would fall apart if you tried to crystallize them.
  • Fast and cheap. About ten mixtures and one instrument you probably already have.
  • Any additive signal will do: absorbance, fluorescence, NMR, heat, mass, conductance, potential, MS intensity (Section 3).
  • More than a formula. The same data give the complex’s molar absorptivity and an estimate of its formation constant.
  • Sets up other experiments. Knowing the ratio tells you how much reagent an assay needs (Application 1), which model to fit in a binding study, and which species to write in a mechanism.
  • Teaches stoichiometry visually: limiting reagent, excess reagent and equilibrium all show up in one plot.7,13
Section 3

Types of measurement

Job’s method does not care how you detect the complex, only that the corrected signal is proportional to the amount of complex and adds up linearly from each species. That opens the door to almost any physical measurement. Each card gives the principle, the quantity you plot, and an example from the literature.

1 · Spectrophotometric (UV–visible)

The classic choice. The complex absorbs at a wavelength where M and L absorb little (or a known amount). Beer’s law makes absorbance linear in concentration.

\[ Y = A_{\text{obs}} - b(\varepsilon_{\text{M}}c_{\text{M}} + \varepsilon_{\text{L}}c_{\text{L}}) \]
Literature: Job used absorbance to study ion association in his 1928 work.1 Vosburgh and Cooper (1941) ran Job series for nickel(II) with ethylenediamine at several wavelengths; because each complex absorbs differently, different wavelengths revealed [Ni(en)]2+, [Ni(en)2]2+ and [Ni(en)3]2+.4

Watch for: deviations from Beer’s law, overlapping spectra, and reactant absorbance that must be subtracted.

2 · Fluorescence

Many sensors switch their emission on or off when they bind a target. Emission intensity is proportional to concentration in dilute solution, and fluorescence is sensitive enough to work at micromolar levels.

\[ Y = F_{\text{obs}} - (F_{\text{M}} + F_{\text{L}}) \]
Literature: Thapa et al. (2021) built diaza-crown ether naphthalimide “turn-on” sensors for single barium ions (for neutrinoless double-beta-decay experiments). A fluorescence Job plot showed that each sensor binds one Ba2+ (1:1).11

Watch for: inner-filter effects at higher absorbance, quenching, and self-association of the dye.

3 · NMR spectroscopy

In fast exchange, an observed chemical shift is an average of free and bound forms. The shift change \(\Delta\delta\) is proportional to the fraction bound, so multiply it by the mole fraction of the species you are watching (here a host H that binds a guest G) to get a quantity proportional to the amount of complex.

\[ Y = \Delta\delta_{\text{obs}}\,x_{\text{H}} \;\propto\; [\mathrm{HG}] \]
Literature: Collum and co-workers used 6Li NMR Job plots to work out how lithium enolates of simple ketones aggregate in solution: mixing two enolates creates mixed aggregates whose number and proportions reveal the aggregate size.12 (See Application 3.)

Watch for: slow exchange (separate peaks, so integrate instead), and the need to choose a proton whose shift changes clearly.

4 · Gravimetric

When the product is an insoluble solid, filter, dry and weigh it. The mass of precipitate is proportional to the moles of product, so the heaviest precipitate marks the stoichiometric mixture. A quick version measures precipitate height in identical test tubes.

\[ Y = m_{\text{ppt}} = n_{\text{product}} \times \mathcal{M}_{\text{product}} \]
Literature: Furlong, Rubinski and Indralingam (2013) had students mix BaCl2 with (NH4)2HPO4 under neutral, acidic and basic conditions, weigh the dried precipitates, and use continuous-variation plots to show that a different barium phosphate forms in each medium.13

Watch for: mass is an extensive quantity, so total volume and total moles must be identical in every mixture (Section 4). Incomplete drying and co-precipitation add error.

5 · Thermometric (calorimetric)

A fast, exothermic reaction releases heat in proportion to the moles that react. Mix the solutions in an insulated cup and record the temperature rise; the hottest mixture is the stoichiometric one.

\[ \Delta T = \frac{-\Delta H_{\text{r}}\; n_{\text{reacted}}}{C_{\text{p,total}}} \]
Literature: In a widely used teaching experiment, sodium hypochlorite (bleach) and sodium thiosulfate are mixed in different proportions at constant total volume; the largest \(\Delta T\) comes at 4 OCl− : 1 S2O32−, which fixes the coefficients:14
\[ \ce{S2O3^2- + 4OCl- + 2OH- -> 2SO4^2- + 4Cl- + H2O} \]

Watch for: heat loss to the surroundings, different starting temperatures, and slow reactions. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) is the modern, more sensitive relative of this approach.

6 · Potentiometric

An ion-selective electrode (or a pH electrode) reports the activity of free metal ion (or of H+ released on binding). The amount bound is what is missing from the total.

\[ E = E^{\circ\prime} + \frac{RT}{zF}\ln[\mathrm{M}]_{\text{free}} \qquad Y = c_{\text{M}} - [\mathrm{M}]_{\text{free}} \]
Literature: Work on copper–citrate solutions measured free Cu2+ with a copper ion-selective electrode and analyzed the mixtures by Job’s method as modified by Vosburgh and Cooper, pointing to a 1:1 copper–citrate complex.15

Watch for: the electrode responds to activity, so ionic strength must be held constant; slow electrode response at low concentrations.

7 · Conductometric

Binding changes the number and mobility of ions: a bulky crown ether wrapped around K+ moves more slowly than bare K+. The departure of the measured conductivity from the no-reaction (additive) value tracks the complex.

\[ Y = \big[x\,\kappa_{\text{M}} + (1-x)\,\kappa_{\text{L}}\big] - \kappa_{\text{obs}} \]
Literature: Çalışır and Çiçek (2019) adapted the Job plot to conductivity measurements to study how benzo-thio and benzo-oxo crown ethers bind metal ions such as Na+ and K+ in 50% acetonitrile–water.16

Watch for: only works when complexation changes ion mobility noticeably; temperature must be tightly controlled (conductivity rises about 2% per °C).

8 · Mass spectrometry (ESI-MS)

Electrospray ionization carries intact non-covalent complexes into the gas phase. The intensity of the complex ion, relative to the free host, stands in for its concentration.

\[ Y = \Delta I_{\text{rel}} \times x_{\text{host}} \]
Literature: Mathieson, Cooper and Cronin (2014) introduced ESI-MS Job plots; for 12-crown-4 with Li+ the plot peaked at a mole fraction of 0.5 (1:1), and anion binding by a receptor was cross-checked against an NMR Job plot.17

Watch for: different species ionize with different efficiencies, and complexes can fall apart or form in the spray.

9 · Capillary electrophoresis

CE separates complexes by charge and size, so each species gives its own peak. A separate Job plot for each peak can reveal several complexes in the same mixtures.

\[ Y_i = \text{peak area of species } i \]
Literature: Boland and Stone (2013) used CE Job plots on nickel(II) with the chelating agent DTPA; three CE peaks gave maxima at Ni : DTPA = 1:1, 1:2 and a previously unreported 3:2.18

Watch for: complexes must survive the separation (kinetically inert), and peak areas need calibration.

10 · Absorbance with variable path length (biochemistry)

Very tight binding needs very dilute solutions, where absorbance is tiny. Lengthening the cell path as the concentration drops keeps the signal measurable.

\[ A = \varepsilon\, b\, c \quad (b \text{ raised as } c \text{ falls}) \]
Literature: Davydov, Fernando and Halpert (2006) developed variable-path-length and counter-flow versions of the continuous-variation method and applied them to substrate binding by cytochromes P450 3A4 and P450eryF.19

Watch for: proteins can aggregate or have several binding sites, which blurs the peak.

11 · Other properties

Any property that is additive for the pure components and changes on complex formation can be used, including:

  • freezing point, density, viscosity, refractive index, dielectric constant or surface tension of binary liquid mixtures (the earliest uses)2,3,5
  • circular dichroism (chiral complexes) and IR band intensities
  • polarographic or amperometric current of an electroactive species
  • initial reaction rate, when the complex is the active catalyst
  • solubility of a sparingly soluble partner

Section 4

Why the total volume must stay constant

The derivation in Section 2 assumed that the total concentration \(c_{\text{T}} = c_{\text{M}} + c_{\text{L}}\) is the same in every mixture. Keeping the total volume constant, with equimolar stocks, is how you make that true in the lab. Change the volume from one mixture to the next and the signal starts reporting dilution as well as composition.4,7

What constant volume guarantees

Each mixture is made from equimolar stocks (\(c_0\)) and brought to a total volume \(V_{\text{T}}\):

\[ c_{\text{M}} = \frac{c_0 V_{\text{M}}}{V_{\text{T}}} \qquad c_{\text{L}} = \frac{c_0 V_{\text{L}}}{V_{\text{T}}} \]
\[ c_{\text{T}} = c_{\text{M}} + c_{\text{L}} = c_0\,\frac{V_{\text{M}} + V_{\text{L}}}{V_{\text{T}}} = c_0 \quad\text{only when } V_{\text{M}} + V_{\text{L}} = V_{\text{T}} \text{ in every mixture} \]

If mixture \(i\) instead ends up in its own final volume \(V_{\text{f},i}\), its total concentration becomes

\[ c_{\text{T},i} = c_0\,\frac{V_{\text{T}}}{V_{\text{f},i}} \]

and its signal is scaled by a factor that has nothing to do with stoichiometry.

Four reasons it matters

  1. Composition is the only variable. At constant \(c_{\text{T}}\), any change in signal comes from the change in \(x\), not from dilution.
  2. Mole fraction equals volume fraction (\(x_{\text{L}} = V_{\text{L}}/V_{\text{T}}\)), so you can set \(x\) with a burette.
  3. Extensive signals stay comparable. Precipitate mass, heat released and precipitate height scale with the total amount. Every mixture must contain the same total moles in the same volume.
  4. The baseline is a straight line. The no-reaction signal \(x\,Y_{\text{M}} + (1-x)\,Y_{\text{L}}\) is linear in \(x\) only at constant \(c_{\text{T}}\), so the correction in Section 2 works.

The same holds for everything else in the medium: buffer, ionic strength, solvent composition and temperature should be identical in every flask.7,27

Let the volume drift across the series and watch the formula go wrong

The drift makes the volume of mixture \(i\) equal to \(V(1 + d\,(2x_{\text{L}} - 1))\): mixtures rich in M get less volume and mixtures rich in L get more (or the reverse, for negative \(d\)). Straight lines are fitted through the three points at each end, the way a student would extrapolate, and their intersection gives the reported formula.

Set \(d = 0\) to recover the correct plot. Then try \(d = \pm 0.3\) (a 30% volume error at each end): for ML the lines now cross near 0.41 or 0.59, which you would read as M3L2 or M2L3. Notice that the drift pushes absorbance and precipitate mass in opposite directions.

Why the end lines tilt

Near \(x_{\text{L}} = 0\) the signal rises as \(c_{\text{T},i}\,x\), and near \(x_{\text{L}} = 1\) it falls as \(c_{\text{T},i}(1-x)\). If \(c_{\text{T}}\) (or total moles) drifts across the series, one line becomes steeper and the other shallower, so they cross at the wrong place.

  • Concentration signals (absorbance, fluorescence): a bigger final volume dilutes, so the signal drops where the volume is largest and the crossing moves away from that end.
  • Amount signals (mass, heat): a bigger delivered volume holds more moles, so the signal grows where the volume is largest and the crossing moves toward that end.

When the reaction is weak

For a sharp triangle (complete reaction), the highest point can survive a modest drift, but the extrapolated lines do not. For a rounded curve (weak binding), even the highest point moves toward the more concentrated end, because the amount of complex depends on concentration more than linearly.

When the stocks are not equimolar

If the ligand stock is \(p\) times as concentrated as the metal stock, the peak in volume fraction moves, but the ratio can still be recovered:

\[ \frac{n}{m} = \frac{p\,(1 - f_{\max})}{f_{\max}} \qquad f = \frac{V_{\text{M}}}{V_{\text{T}}} \]

Constant total volume is still required. Only the conversion from volume to mole fraction changes.4

Hidden volume changes

  • Mixing solvents (ethanol + water) gives a total volume smaller than the sum: dilute to the mark, don’t just add volumes.
  • Adding a reagent “to each tube” (buffer, reductant, indicator) without adding the same volume to all of them.
  • Evaporation from open tubes while slow reactions equilibrate.
  • Warming: solution volume rises about 0.02% per °C, so compare at one temperature.27
Section 5

Three applications in chemistry, and where the method breaks down

The plots for Applications 1 and 2 are calculated from constants published for those systems, so their shapes are what an experiment would give under the stated conditions. The plot for Application 3 shows the ideal statistical distribution that real data are compared against.

Application 1 · Analytical chemistry

Designing an assay: iron with 1,10-phenanthroline

Iron(II) and 1,10-phenanthroline form the intensely orange-red ferroin ion, the basis of a standard method for determining iron in water, foods and pharmaceuticals.20

\[ \ce{Fe^2+ + 3phen -> [Fe(phen)3]^2+} \]

The Job plot peaks at \(x_{\text{Fe}} = 0.25\), so three phenanthrolines bind each iron. The binding is so strong (\(\beta_3 \approx 10^{21}\ \text{M}^{-3}\)) that the plot is an almost perfect triangle; the slopes of its two sides give \(\varepsilon\) directly.

Why it matters: knowing the ratio is 3:1 tells the analyst to add phenanthroline in a clear excess (well over three per iron), so that all of the iron is converted and absorbance is proportional to iron concentration (Beer’s law calibration).

Application 2 · Supramolecular and pharmaceutical chemistry

Host–guest inclusion: cyclodextrins carrying drugs

Cyclodextrins (CDs) are ring-shaped sugars with a water-repelling cavity. Drugs that are poorly soluble in water can slip into the cavity, which raises their solubility and stability. Formulation chemists need the stoichiometry before they can choose a binding model or a dose.

\[ \beta\text{-CD} + \text{G} \rightleftharpoons \beta\text{-CD}\cdot\text{G} \qquad K = \frac{[\text{CD}\cdot\text{G}]}{[\text{CD}][\text{G}]} \]

Iacovino et al. (2012) studied hydroxymethylferrocene, an anticancer candidate, with β-cyclodextrin. A Job plot established 1:1 inclusion, and NMR and UV–visible titrations gave \(K \approx 478\) and \(541\ \text{M}^{-1}\).21

Lesson from the plot: with modest \(K\), the curve is rounded and the amount of complex depends strongly on \(c_{\text{T}}\), yet both curves peak at 0.50, exactly as the derivation predicts for a single complex. Rounded curves are typical of host–guest chemistry, which is why reviewers recommend confirming Job results with full titrations.9,10

Application 3 · Organometallic chemistry

Counting the units in an aggregate: lithium enolates

Organolithium reagents and lithium enolates rarely exist as single molecules in solution; they cluster into dimers, tetramers or hexamers, and the size controls their reactivity. An ordinary Job plot cannot see the size (a 1:1 ratio fits a dimer and a tetramer alike).

Collum’s group solved this by mixing two different enolates, A and B, at constant total concentration. They exchange to form mixed aggregates, each with its own 6Li NMR signal.12 The number of species and where each one peaks reveal \(N\):

\[ P(\mathrm{A}_k\mathrm{B}_{N-k}) = \binom{N}{k}\,X_{\text{A}}^{\,k}\,(1-X_{\text{A}})^{N-k} \]

Read the plot: a dimer gives 3 species (A2, AB, B2), a tetramer 5, a hexamer 7. Departures from the ideal curves show which mixed aggregates are favored.5,12

Section 5 · Limitations

When a Job plot can mislead you

The neat result \(x_{\max} = n/(m+n)\) rests on assumptions. When they fail, the peak can still look convincing while pointing to the wrong formula. Recent reviews argue that in host–guest chemistry this happens often enough that Job plots should not be used alone.9,10

At small \(K_2\) the peak sits at 0.50 (ML); at large \(K_2\) it reaches 0.67 (ML2). In between, a peak near 0.58 suggests a “1.4:1 complex” that does not exist. Vosburgh and Cooper’s remedy was to repeat the series at several wavelengths.4

1 · Only one complex may dominate

Stepwise equilibria (ML, ML2, ML3) or competing species (M2L and ML) shift the peak to an intermediate, meaningless position (see above).4,9

2 · Weak binding blurs the answer

When \(\beta c_{\text{T}}^{\,m+n-1}\) is small, the curve flattens, extrapolation becomes guesswork, and noise can move the apparent maximum. Raising \(c_{\text{T}}\) helps only if solubility allows.8,9

3 · Ratio, not size

ML, M2L2 and M3L3 all peak at 0.50. Aggregation number needs a separate measurement (Application 3, MS, colligative properties).5,12

4 · The signal must be linear and additive

Beer’s-law deviations, fluorescence inner-filter effects, self-association of a dye, or NMR signals in intermediate exchange all break the proportionality between signal and complex.7,10

5 · The medium must not take part

pH changes alter ligand protonation and metal hydrolysis; buffers and counter-ions can bind the metal. Ionic strength must be held constant, especially for potentiometric and conductometric work.7,27

6 · Equilibrium must be reached

Slow reactions, aging precipitates and kinetically inert complexes need time; measuring too early distorts the curve.7

7 · Constant total concentration is restrictive

You sample only one \(c_{\text{T}}\). Fitting a binding constant reliably usually needs a titration over a wider range (hold one partner fixed and add the other), analyzed by global nonlinear fitting.9,10

8 · Practical limits

Reactant absorbance must be measured and subtracted; spectral overlap limits wavelength choice; high-ratio complexes (ML4 to ML6) peak too close together to tell apart without dense, precise data.5,7

Section 6

Other ways to find stoichiometry, compared

Job’s method is one of several tools for finding how many of each partner a compound contains. Some, like the mole-ratio and slope-ratio methods, are close cousins that vary the composition differently. Others fit whole titration curves, measure heat, weigh ions in a mass spectrometer, or isolate the compound and analyze it directly. No single method is best for every system; the strongest conclusions come from two methods that agree.5,9,10,31

Close relatives of Job’s method

Vary the composition a different way

Mole-ratio method

Keep the metal concentration fixed and add more and more ligand. The signal rises while metal is still free, then levels off once every metal is complexed. The break comes at the ratio in the formula. Introduced by Yoe and Jones (1944) for the iron–Tiron complex.28

\[ A = \varepsilon b\,[\mathrm{ML}_n] \quad\text{levels off at}\quad \frac{c_{\text{L}}}{c_{\text{M}}} = n \]
AdvantagesSimple; stepwise complexes (ML, ML2, …) can show up as separate breaks; the plateau gives \(\varepsilon\) directly.
LimitationsWeak complexes give a gradual curve with no clear break; needs a soluble excess of ligand; ligand absorbance grows across the series.
Slope-ratio method

Run two series. In one, ligand is in large excess and the metal concentration is varied; in the other, metal is in excess and the ligand is varied. Each gives a straight line, and the ratio of the slopes is the ratio in the formula (Harvey and Manning, 1950).29

\[ \frac{\text{slope}_{\,\text{M varied}}}{\text{slope}_{\,\text{L varied}}} = \frac{\varepsilon b/m}{\varepsilon b/n} = \frac{n}{m} \]
AdvantagesThe large excess drives formation to completion, so it suits weak complexes that give rounded Job plots; slopes are easy to measure precisely.
LimitationsAssumes the same single complex forms in both series; with metal in excess, lower complexes such as ML can form instead of MLn; needs Beer’s law over a wide range.
Job’s method (for comparison)

Keep the total concentration fixed and vary the proportion (Sections 1–4).1,4

\[ x_{\text{L,max}} = \frac{n}{m+n} \]
AdvantagesPeak position does not depend on \(K\) for a single complex; one short series; no reagent in large excess.
LimitationsMisleading when several complexes coexist; flat curves for weak binding; gives only the ratio, not the size.9,10
Fitting a whole binding curve

Let a model choose the stoichiometry

Titration with nonlinear fitting

Hold the host concentration fixed, add guest over a wide range, and fit the whole curve to 1:1, 1:2 and 2:1 binding models at once. The model whose residuals scatter randomly, with sensible constants, wins. This is now the recommended approach in supramolecular chemistry.31,9,10

\[ \Delta Y_{\text{obs}} = \Delta Y_{\text{HG}}\,\frac{[\mathrm{HG}]}{[\mathrm{H}]_0} + 2\,\Delta Y_{\text{HG}_2}\,\frac{[\mathrm{HG_2}]}{[\mathrm{H}]_0} + \cdots \]
AdvantagesGives stoichiometry and every binding constant together, with uncertainties; uses data across 20–80% saturation; handles mixtures of complexes.
LimitationsNeeds fitting software and careful model comparison; more complex models always fit a little better, so overfitting is a risk; needs good data over a wide range.
Benesi–Hildebrand (double-reciprocal) plot

A linearized titration for 1:1 complexes, first used for iodine with aromatic hydrocarbons (Benesi and Hildebrand, 1949). A straight line is taken as evidence of 1:1 binding.30

\[ \frac{1}{\Delta A} = \frac{1}{b\,\Delta\varepsilon\,K\,[\mathrm{H}]_0}\cdot\frac{1}{[\mathrm{G}]_0} + \frac{1}{b\,\Delta\varepsilon\,[\mathrm{H}]_0} \]
AdvantagesNeeds only a ruler or a spreadsheet; gives \(K\) and \(\Delta\varepsilon\) from slope and intercept.
LimitationsAssumes 1:1 from the start, so it cannot decide stoichiometry well; taking reciprocals distorts the errors; curvature from other complexes is easy to miss.31
Potentiometric formation curve (Bjerrum)

Titrate a metal–ligand solution with acid or base and use the pH to find the free ligand concentration. The average number of ligands per metal, \(\bar{n}\), climbs in steps toward the maximum coordination number. Bjerrum used it for metal–ammonia complexes.32

\[ \bar{n} = \frac{c_{\text{L}} - [\mathrm{L}]}{c_{\text{M}}} = \frac{\sum_i i\,\beta_i[\mathrm{L}]^i}{1 + \sum_i \beta_i[\mathrm{L}]^i} \]
AdvantagesFinds every stepwise complex (ML to MLn) and all stepwise constants; the standard source of the stability constants in data tables.
LimitationsLigand must be a weak acid or base; needs careful electrode calibration and constant ionic strength; heavy data treatment.
Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

Inject one partner into the other and measure the heat of each injection. A fit of the heat curve returns the stoichiometry \(n\), \(K\) and \(\Delta H\) in one experiment (Wiseman et al., 1989).33

\[ Q = n\,[\mathrm{M}]_{\text{t}}\,V_0\,\Delta H\,\theta \qquad c = nK[\mathrm{M}]_{\text{t}} \approx 1\text{–}1000 \]
AdvantagesNo label or chromophore needed; gives the full thermodynamics (\(\Delta G\), \(\Delta H\), \(\Delta S\)); widely used for proteins and drugs.
Limitations\(n\) absorbs any error in the stated concentrations, so impure samples give fractional \(n\); needs measurable heat and the right \(c\) window; expensive instrument.
Measuring the size of the species

Methods that can tell ML from M2L2

Mass spectrometry (ESI-MS)

Electrospray transfers intact complexes to the gas phase; the mass-to-charge ratio and isotope pattern give the full composition, including the degree of aggregation.17

\[ \frac{m}{z} = \frac{M_{\text{complex}}}{z} \]
AdvantagesGives absolute composition, not just a ratio; needs micrograms; sees several species at once.
LimitationsThe gas phase is not the solution: complexes can fall apart or form during spraying, and ionization efficiencies differ.
Diffusion NMR (DOSY)

Larger species diffuse more slowly. Measuring the diffusion coefficient of each NMR signal gives the size of the species in solution and hence its aggregation number.34

\[ D = \frac{k_{\text{B}}T}{6\pi\eta\,r_{\text{H}}} \]
AdvantagesMeasured in solution, without isolating anything; separates species in a mixture by size.
LimitationsSize estimates assume a shape (usually a sphere); needs fairly concentrated samples; fast exchange averages the sizes.
Colligative properties

Freezing-point depression, boiling-point elevation and vapor-pressure osmometry count dissolved particles, so they give the molar mass, and with it the aggregation number.27

\[ \Delta T_{\text{f}} = K_{\text{f}}\, m_{\text{particles}} \]
AdvantagesSimple, classic equipment; directly reports the number of particles.
LimitationsNeeds high concentrations and nearly ideal solutions; small effects; cannot separate a mixture of species.
Other approaches

Solubility, isolation and structure

Phase-solubility analysis

Shake excess of a poorly soluble compound (often a drug) with increasing concentrations of a ligand such as a cyclodextrin and measure how much dissolves. A straight line (AL type) indicates 1:1; upward curvature (AP type) indicates higher-order complexes (Higuchi and Connors, 1965).35

\[ K_{1:1} = \frac{\text{slope}}{S_0\,(1 - \text{slope})} \]
AdvantagesThe standard method in pharmaceutics; measures the solubility gain that formulators care about.
LimitationsOnly for poorly soluble partners; equilibration can take days; curve shapes can be ambiguous.
Isolation, elemental analysis and X-ray crystallography

Crystallize the compound, then find its mass percentages of C, H, N and metal (giving the empirical formula) or solve its crystal structure (giving every atom’s position).27

\[ \text{mol ratio} = \frac{\%\,\text{X}/A_{\text{r}}(\text{X})}{\%\,\text{Y}/A_{\text{r}}(\text{Y})} \]
AdvantagesDefinitive: an X-ray structure gives the molecular formula, geometry and bonding.
LimitationsNeeds a pure, crystalline solid; the species that crystallizes may not be the one that dominates in solution.

Choosing a method

  1. Strong, single complex, colored: a Job or mole-ratio plot is quick and reliable.1,28
  2. Weak complex: use the slope-ratio method, or better, a titration analyzed by nonlinear fitting.29,31
  3. Several complexes possible: fit titration data to competing models, or use Bjerrum potentiometry for metal–ligand systems.31,32
  4. Size or aggregation in doubt: add mass spectrometry, diffusion NMR or an ensemble Job plot.12,17,34
  5. Thermodynamics wanted: ITC gives \(n\), \(K\) and \(\Delta H\) together.33
  6. Final proof of a molecular formula: isolate the compound for elemental analysis or X-ray crystallography, then check that the same species exists in solution.27
